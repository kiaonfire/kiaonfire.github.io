Respect & representation · Course & Program Design

Are marginalised perspectives embedded meaningfully?

A practical Inclusive Teaching Framework action.

Framework practice

Respect · Curriculum

Marginalised perspectives are embedded as substantive, not token

Back to Curriculum

Why this matters

Teaching is never neutral. Choices about readings, cases, examples, guest speakers, classroom norms and professional pathways tell students whose lives and knowledge are considered normal, legitimate or peripheral. When students cannot recognise themselves or their communities in the discipline, participation can start to feel risky or pointless.

What this looks like

Common signs

  • The course may centre only a narrow set of identities, voices or professional pathways.
  • Students may encounter norms that quietly position some ways of being as more legitimate than others.
  • Participation can become more cautious when students expect to be stereotyped, ignored or made responsible for representing a whole group.

What students may experience

  • “People like me do not seem to exist in this field.”
  • “We only appear when the course is discussing disadvantage or a problem.”
  • “To contribute here, I may need to ignore part of myself or explain my community to others.”

Steps to address it

1
Build these perspectives into core concepts, not sidebars or optional extras.
2
Connect them to the main learning questions of the course.
3
Avoid including a perspective only when discussing disadvantage or diversity itself.

Example in practice

A planning course treats First Nations knowledges as integral to land use and design, rather than a stand-alone “special topic”.
