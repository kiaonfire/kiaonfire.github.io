What would university look like if it were designed around the dignity of the people who actually study and work here?

People would not have to fit one narrow model of the “ideal” student or worker in order to participate. Difference would be anticipated as part of university life, rather than treated as an exception that people must repeatedly explain.

Teaching, systems and relationships would make room for people to learn, contribute and exercise agency without unnecessary self-erasure, avoidable friction or having to carry the cost of inclusion alone.

Inclusive teaching comes together when six pieces reinforce each other.

No single piece is enough on its own. Together they connect human worth, practical teaching design, institutional conditions, evidence and responsibility for change.

Design around people of equal worth

Change is needed when participation asks people to hide important parts of themselves, absorb avoidable costs, or fit a narrow model of who belongs at university.

Make the conditions visible

Respect, Equity, Accessibility and Language help us examine what participation requires across curriculum, teaching and assessment.

Turn principles into everyday practice

UDL, accessible assessment, flexible participation, inclusive curriculum, relational teaching, transparent communication and co-design make inclusion practical.

Create the conditions staff need

Workload, staffing, policy, timetabling, employment structures, physical environments, leadership and resources determine what staff can realistically sustain.

See where people are thriving or being excluded

Student voice, staff voice, lived experience, institutional data, research and legislation reveal patterns, test assumptions and show where change is needed.

Make feedback lead somewhere

Decision rights, clear responses, resourcing, leadership responsibility and measurable systemic change turn good intentions into durable improvement.

The Inclusive Teaching Framework is the practical bridge between these ideas and everyday teaching. It helps staff identify where to act now, while also showing when a barrier needs change beyond the individual classroom.

Why this matters in practice

Inclusive teaching matters because widening participation is now central to the future of higher education, and because access alone is not enough without the conditions to succeed.

Mass participation requires inclusive design

The Australian Universities Accord recommended participation parity by 2050 for students from underrepresented backgrounds, and the Australian Government has set a national target for 80% tertiary attainment by 2050. Reaching ambitions like these is not only about opening more places — universities also need learning conditions that enable a much wider range of people to participate and succeed.

Who does a more inclusive university need to work for?

It needs to work for students who may be disabled or neurodivergent, balancing paid work, studying part-time, caring for others, commuting long distances, managing limited financial resources, entering through non-traditional pathways, or bringing identities and experiences that have historically been marginalised. Access without the conditions for success is not enough.

Inclusive teaching can expand what university makes possible in people’s lives.

Participation

Students are more able to enter learning, contribute and stay connected when avoidable barriers are reduced.

Belonging

Recognition and respectful relationships help people experience university as somewhere they have a legitimate place.

Capability

When assessment and teaching focus more clearly on the learning that matters, universities are less likely to confuse privilege, familiarity or spare time with ability.

Possibility

Successful participation can expand knowledge, confidence, professional pathways, community connection and people’s capacity to shape their own futures.

Inclusive teaching opens up learning to everyone.

University should work for the people who are actually here.

Inclusive teaching reduces avoidable barriers, makes expectations visible and creates room for different lives, identities and ways of participating. It is not only about opening the door — it is about whether people can participate with dignity, build capability and succeed.

What inclusive teaching asks us to do

Inclusive practice works best when everyday teaching choices and the conditions around them reinforce each other.

Design for the students who are actually here

Anticipate different identities, responsibilities, bodies, resources and ways of learning instead of requiring people to fit one ‘ideal’ student model.

Build inclusion into everyday practice

Use Respect, Equity, Accessibility and Language to examine curriculum, teaching and assessment, then make barriers easier to avoid rather than repair later.

Share responsibility for change

Student and staff voice, data and lived experience help identify barriers. Some can be changed in a course; others need time, systems, resourcing or institutional action.
