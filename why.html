Why this matters

Inclusive teaching opens up learning to everyone.

Universities should be places where people are able to learn as whole human beings. Inclusive teaching deliberately designs education so students are treated as people of equal worth — able to enter learning, see themselves and their communities recognised, experience care and connection, retain autonomy, contribute knowledge and exercise meaningful agency.

A different starting point

What would university look like if it were designed around the dignity of the people who actually study and work here?

People would not have to fit one narrow model of the “ideal” student or worker in order to participate. Difference would be anticipated as part of university life, rather than treated as an exception that people must repeatedly explain.

Teaching, systems and relationships would make room for people to learn, contribute and exercise agency without unnecessary self-erasure, avoidable friction or having to carry the cost of inclusion alone.

The pieces that make inclusion possible

Inclusive teaching comes together when six pieces reinforce each other.

No single piece is enough on its own. Together they connect human worth, practical teaching design, institutional conditions, evidence and responsibility for change.

Dignity

Design around people of equal worth

Change is needed when participation asks people to hide important parts of themselves, absorb avoidable costs, or fit a narrow model of who belongs at university.

Inclusive Teaching Framework

Make the conditions visible

Respect, Equity, Accessibility and Language help us examine what participation requires across curriculum, teaching and assessment. The four areas can be remembered as REAL.

Inclusive design

Turn principles into everyday practice

UDL, accessible assessment, flexible participation, inclusive curriculum, relational teaching, transparent communication and co-design make inclusion practical.

Organisational conditions

Create the conditions staff need

Workload, staffing, policy, timetabling, employment structures, physical environments, leadership and resources determine what staff can realistically sustain.

Evidence & voice

See where people are thriving or being excluded

Student voice, staff voice, lived experience, institutional data, research and legislation reveal patterns, test assumptions and show where change is needed.

Accountability & change

Make feedback lead somewhere

Decision rights, clear responses, resourcing, leadership responsibility and measurable systemic change turn good intentions into durable improvement.

The Inclusive Teaching Framework is the practical bridge between these pieces and everyday teaching. It helps staff identify where to act now, while also showing when a barrier needs change beyond the individual classroom.

The vehicle for improvement

The Inclusive Teaching Framework turns these ideas into questions we can act on.

It is the practical vehicle for improving curriculum, teaching and assessment: helping staff find changes they can make now, connect those changes to a wider picture of inclusion, and recognise when organisational change is also needed.

R

Respect & representation

Whose knowledge, identities, communities and possible futures are recognised as legitimate here?

E

Equity

Can students with different lives, resources and responsibilities realistically participate?

A

Accessibility

Are spaces, materials, activities and systems usable without avoidable friction?

L

Language & literacies

Have the rules, expectations and disciplinary ways of communicating been made visible and learnable?

Opening university to more people

Mass participation requires inclusive design.

The Australian Universities Accord argues that higher education must become far more equitable if Australia is to expand tertiary participation. It calls for equal participation at university for under-represented groups by 2050, including First Nations students, students from low socioeconomic backgrounds, regional and remote students, and students with disability.

The Australian Government has also set a target for 80% of working-age people to hold a tertiary qualification by 2050. Reaching ambitions like these is not only about opening more places. Universities need to be designed so a much wider range of people can enter, participate and succeed.

Who does a more inclusive university need to work for?

Students may be disabled or neurodivergent, work substantial hours, study part-time, care for others, commute long distances, have limited financial resources, enter through non-traditional pathways, or bring identities and experiences that have historically been marginalised.

Access without the conditions for success is not enough.

Why success matters

Inclusive teaching can expand what university makes possible in people's lives.

Participation

Students are more able to enter learning, contribute and stay connected when avoidable barriers are reduced.

Belonging

Recognition and respectful relationships help people experience university as somewhere they have a legitimate place.

Capability

When assessment and teaching focus more clearly on the learning that matters, universities are less likely to confuse privilege, familiarity or spare time with ability.

Possibility

Successful participation can expand knowledge, confidence, professional pathways, community connection and people's capacity to shape their own futures.
