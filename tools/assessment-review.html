Assessment review

Key tenet These dimensions interact. A student may face barriers across several categories simultaneously, and the strongest inclusive practice addresses them in combination rather than ticking each off independently. When using this tool, consider your course or teaching as a whole and look for patterns across dimensions rather than treating each row in isolation.

Use the levels as a shared review language, not as a rigid compliance score. Some areas may be stronger than others, and the goal is to identify where course design, teaching and assessment reinforce the same barrier.

Section 3: Assessment

How student learning is measured, including task design, conditions, criteria, and the assumptions built into how evidence of learning is defined and evaluated.

Review reminder: after selecting levels in this section, look across the four dimensions to see whether the same underlying barrier appears in more than one row.
R

Representation of, and respect for, cultures and identities

Whether assessment recognises diverse perspectives as valid evidence of learning and avoids narrow cultural assumptions.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

Tasks reflect Anglo cultural assumptions; non-Western knowledge and diverse identities are absent or tokenised; professional norms remain narrow and dominant.

In practice: Tasks use culturally narrow cases, assume one professional norm and position non-dominant knowledge as irrelevant or less rigorous.

Developing

Some movement

Cultural diversity is acknowledged in some tasks but student choice, assessment criteria and placement criteria review are only partial or inconsistent.

In practice: Students may select from several topics, but the criteria and exemplars still reward one cultural or disciplinary model.

Proficient

Strong practice

Tasks are audited for cultural bias; First Nations, diverse international and other non-Western knowledge are accepted as valid evidence; some topic or choice flexibility exists.

In practice: Tasks and criteria are reviewed for bias, several perspectives are accepted as valid evidence and examples reflect varied communities.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Students may choose topics and approaches that reflect their backgrounds and communities; diverse identities are treated as valid evidence and students help review assessment contexts.

In practice: Students and relevant communities help shape contexts, topics and exemplars, while the integrity of the learning outcomes remains explicit.

How to move this area forward

No/Poor → Developing
  • Review the names, contexts, communities and assumed values in one assessment task.
  • Remove a stereotype or deficit-based scenario.
  • Check whether criteria reward cultural familiarity rather than the intended capability.

Developing → Proficient
  • Offer bounded topic or context choice aligned to the same outcomes.
  • Use several annotated exemplars rather than one ideal model.
  • Review marking criteria with colleagues or students for hidden cultural assumptions.

Proficient → Excellent
  • Partner with students to redesign cases or assessment contexts.
  • Include community or practitioner input where ethically and disciplinarily appropriate.
  • Moderate how varied perspectives and expression are assessed.

  • Maintain consent and safeguards for sensitive or difficult histories.

E

Equity of access and participation

Whether assessment conditions are fair for students with different responsibilities, locations, health conditions and resource constraints.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

Assessment is rigid: no exemplars or flexibility; tasks incur additional costs; assessment assumes constant access to time, money, space and technology.

In practice: Assessment assumes stable health, time, money, transport, technology and private space, with rigid deadlines and one high-stakes attempt.

Developing

Some movement

Some flexibility is available on request, but criteria and conditions still partially assume a narrow ideal student and placement assessment remains inconsistent.

In practice: Extensions or alternatives are available by request, but processes are burdensome and the standard design remains inflexible.

Proficient

Strong practice

Flexible deadlines and staged submissions are available as standard; tasks consider health, caring, distance and prior learning circumstances where possible.

In practice: Tasks are cost-conscious, staged and supported by clear criteria, with reasonable flexibility available as standard.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Flexible deadlines and cost-neutral tasks are standard; assessment remains fair for students managing different conditions, including health, remote location, caring and financial pressure.

In practice: Assessment burden is coordinated across the program, indirect discrimination is actively reviewed and recurring conditions are redesigned.

How to move this area forward

No/Poor → Developing
  • List every cost, deadline, location, technology and continuous-attendance assumption in the task.
  • Add one low-stakes checkpoint or formative opportunity.
  • Clarify the extension, absence and adjustment process.

List every cost, deadline, location, technology and continuous-attendance assumption in the task. Add one low-stakes checkpoint or formative opportunity. Clarify the extension, absence and adjustment process.

Developing → Proficient
  • Build reasonable deadline or submission flexibility into the baseline where policy allows.
  • Remove costs or fixed conditions not essential to the learning outcome.
  • Review whether students can recover learning or marks after health-related absence.

Build reasonable deadline or submission flexibility into the baseline where policy allows. Remove costs or fixed conditions not essential to the learning outcome. Review whether students can recover learning or marks after health-related absence.

Proficient → Excellent
  • Map assessment timing, cost and workload across the program.
  • Review outcomes and adjustment themes for inequitable patterns.
  • Coordinate consistent flexibility and support across teaching teams.

Map assessment timing, cost and workload across the program. Review outcomes and adjustment themes for inequitable patterns. Coordinate consistent flexibility and support across teaching teams.

Maintain, evaluate and share
  • Evaluate the effect of flexibility on fairness and learning.
  • Keep indirect discrimination under formal review.
  • Share sustainable designs and unresolved constraints through governance.

Evaluate the effect of flexibility on fairness and learning. Keep indirect discrimination under formal review. Share sustainable designs and unresolved constraints through governance.

A

Accessibility and agency of materials and learning pathways

Whether the assessment format itself creates avoidable barriers and whether students can demonstrate learning in more than one meaningful way.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

Tasks assume a single format and response mode; no alternative formats or review of access barriers; students have little agency in how they show learning.

In practice: All students must use one format and delivery mode even when it measures skills unrelated to the learning outcome.

Developing

Some movement

Some format variety or adjustments are available on request, but access is not designed in consistently and students still face process barriers.

In practice: Alternative formats may be approved individually, but the process requires disclosure and the options are not equally supported.

Proficient

Strong practice

Tasks are designed to separate the skill being measured from unnecessary format demands; instructions and submissions are accessible; students can demonstrate learning in at least some varied ways.

In practice: The task separates the assessed capability from unnecessary format demands and offers supported equivalent routes where appropriate.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Assessment proactively removes format barriers; multiple submission modes are standard; students have meaningful agency over topic, mode and ways of demonstrating learning without compromising assessment integrity.

In practice: Assessment optionality is designed, exemplified and moderated across the program, with students exercising meaningful agency without lower standards.

How to move this area forward

No/Poor → Developing
  • Write the genuine learning outcome before naming the required format.
  • Identify which format features are essential and which are inherited habits.
  • Make the instructions, submission process and feedback accessible.

Write the genuine learning outcome before naming the required format. Identify which format features are essential and which are inherited habits. Make the instructions, submission process and feedback accessible.

Developing → Proficient
  • Pilot one bounded equivalent format choice.
  • Provide technical guidance and an exemplar for every option.
  • Use common outcome-aligned criteria and calibrate marking.

Pilot one bounded equivalent format choice. Provide technical guidance and an exemplar for every option. Use common outcome-aligned criteria and calibrate marking.

Proficient → Excellent
  • Review optionality with students and assessors for unintended advantages or barriers.
  • Coordinate format choice across the program rather than isolated tasks.
  • Create alternatives for inaccessible systems and oral or group formats.

Review optionality with students and assessors for unintended advantages or barriers. Coordinate format choice across the program rather than isolated tasks. Create alternatives for inaccessible systems and oral or group formats.

Maintain, evaluate and share
  • Evaluate whether student agency improves learning and access.
  • Share moderation and design resources with other course teams.
  • Continue reviewing whether new formats create new hidden demands.

Evaluate whether student agency improves learning and access. Share moderation and design resources with other course teams. Continue reviewing whether new formats create new hidden demands.

L

Language and literacies

How well the task and criteria scaffold the language, conventions and literacy practices needed for success.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

High language proficiency is assumed; there are no formative tasks to build literacy before summative assessment; instructions are complex or unexplained.

In practice: The task brief is dense, criteria use unexplained terms and students are expected to infer the genre without practice.

Developing

Some movement

Some lower-stakes tasks and examples exist, but language complexity and systemical review of literacy barriers remain limited.

In practice: Examples or support are provided, but language development remains optional, late or disconnected from the task sequence.

Proficient

Strong practice

Low-stakes tasks are sequenced to build language confidence; instructions are reviewed for unnecessary complexity; prior vocabulary gaps are anticipated.

In practice: The task is broken into clear steps, annotated examples are discussed and low-stakes practice develops the required language and literacies.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Low-stakes tasks and annotated exemplars build language development for all students; instructions and criteria are deliberately simplified and multiple expression modes are standard where appropriate.

In practice: Assessment literacy is built across the program, instructions are routinely tested for clarity and varied expression is supported where appropriate.

How to move this area forward

No/Poor → Developing
  • Rewrite the task using direct verbs, clear steps and a short statement of purpose.
  • Define key terms in the criteria.
  • Provide one annotated example before students begin.

Rewrite the task using direct verbs, clear steps and a short statement of purpose. Define key terms in the criteria. Provide one annotated example before students begin.

Developing → Proficient
  • Add a low-stakes planning or practice activity.
  • Discuss how assessors interpret the criteria.
  • Invite students to explain the task back or identify unclear language.

Add a low-stakes planning or practice activity. Discuss how assessors interpret the criteria. Invite students to explain the task back or identify unclear language.

Proficient → Excellent
  • Map assessment and feedback literacy across the program.
  • Use student partnership to test briefs, criteria and exemplars.
  • Align language scaffolds in teaching with the final task.

Map assessment and feedback literacy across the program. Use student partnership to test briefs, criteria and exemplars. Align language scaffolds in teaching with the final task.

Maintain, evaluate and share
  • Evaluate whether scaffolding supports all students without reducing intellectual demand.
  • Share accessible task templates and exemplars.
  • Keep professional and disciplinary language expectations explicit and reviewable.

Evaluate whether scaffolding supports all students without reducing intellectual demand. Share accessible task templates and exemplars. Keep professional and disciplinary language expectations explicit and reviewable.
