Key tenet These dimensions interact. A student may face barriers across several categories simultaneously, and the strongest inclusive practice addresses them in combination rather than ticking each off independently. When using this tool, consider your course or teaching as a whole and look for patterns across dimensions rather than treating each row in isolation.

Use the levels as a shared review language, not as a rigid compliance score. Some areas may be stronger than others, and the goal is to identify where course design, teaching and assessment reinforce the same barrier.

Section 1: Course and Program Design

What is included in the curriculum, how the course or program is structured, and the assumptions built into its design.

Review reminder: after selecting levels in this section, look across the four dimensions to see whether the same underlying barrier appears in more than one row.
R

Representation of, and respect for, cultures and identities

Who and what is centred in the curriculum, and whether diverse students can see themselves and their knowledge treated as legitimate.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

Course centres Western or Anglo knowledge only; diverse perspectives are absent or tokenistic; dominant cultural norms shape placement preparation.

In practice: The reading list is dominated by one intellectual tradition, while diverse perspectives appear only in an optional or isolated week.

Developing

Some movement

Diverse examples are included but inconsistently; cultural diversity is acknowledged in placement preparation but not systematically applied.

In practice: Several diverse authors and cases are added, but they do not yet shape the learning outcomes, assessment or overall story of the course.

Proficient

Strong practice

First Nations, CALD, disability and neurodiverse identities are embedded as substantive content; placement preparation acknowledges the diversity students may encounter or bring.

In practice: Diverse knowledges, communities and professional pathways appear throughout core content, activities and assessment.

Excellent

Embedded practice

The course systematically audits whose knowledge and identities are centred; perspectives from diverse communities are treated as intellectually equal and student feedback actively shapes the curriculum.

In practice: The course or program routinely audits whose knowledge is centred and works with students, communities and relevant experts to revise it.

  • Map the authors, experts, communities and professional roles represented in each module.

  • Align diverse perspectives with learning outcomes, activities and assessment rather than adding them only as examples.
  • Review case studies for stereotypes, extraction or the positioning of communities mainly as problems.

E

Equity of access and participation

Whether course design recognises that students manage different financial, cultural, geographic, health and caring conditions.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

No flexibility for students managing pressure, health or caring responsibilities; materials are costly or inaccessible; course structure assumes a fully unencumbered student.

In practice: The course assumes students can attend at fixed times, purchase all resources, travel freely and manage study without work, care or health disruption.

Developing

Some movement

Flexibility is acknowledged in principle but not consistently designed in; some materials are more affordable but barriers are not systematically reviewed.

In practice: Individual flexibility is sometimes granted, but the standard design and communication still assume an unencumbered student.

Proficient

Strong practice

Flexible pathways are available for students facing financial, health, geographic or caring pressures; affordability and prior learning are considered where possible.

In practice: Costs, scheduling, attendance, placement and workload are reviewed, with flexible pathways available where requirements are not essential.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Flexible pathways are designed in from the outset; barriers such as materials cost, technology expense, placement expectations and rigid structures are proactively reduced.

In practice: Equitable access is designed across the program, with common adjustments mainstreamed, material barriers tracked and systemic issues escalated.

How to move this area forward

No/Poor → Developing
  • List every expected cost, attendance condition, travel requirement and time assumption.
  • Remove one requirement that is not essential to a learning outcome, safety or accreditation.
  • Make existing flexibility and support routes visible before students need to disclose.

List every expected cost, attendance condition, travel requirement and time assumption. Remove one requirement that is not essential to a learning outcome, safety or accreditation. Make existing flexibility and support routes visible before students need to disclose.

Developing → Proficient
  • Review deadline clustering, placement travel, required technology and live-attendance expectations.
  • Create a consistent baseline rather than negotiating the same flexibility differently in every activity.
  • Ask students which material barriers have the greatest effect on participation.

Review deadline clustering, placement travel, required technology and live-attendance expectations. Create a consistent baseline rather than negotiating the same flexibility differently in every activity. Ask students which material barriers have the greatest effect on participation.

Proficient → Excellent
  • Coordinate flexibility and workload across the program.
  • Mainstream common adjustments that benefit many students.
  • Establish an escalation route for barriers controlled by timetabling, placement systems, procurement or policy.

Coordinate flexibility and workload across the program. Mainstream common adjustments that benefit many students. Establish an escalation route for barriers controlled by timetabling, placement systems, procurement or policy.

Maintain, evaluate and share
  • Monitor whether the equitable baseline is used consistently across teaching teams.
  • Review who still needs repeated exceptions and why.
  • Share evidence of resolved and unresolved material barriers through governance.

Monitor whether the equitable baseline is used consistently across teaching teams. Review who still needs repeated exceptions and why. Share evidence of resolved and unresolved material barriers through governance.

A

Accessibility and agency of materials and learning pathways

Whether students can access materials, navigate learning and exercise meaningful choice in how they engage.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

Digital materials lack captions, alt text or screen-reader compatibility; the online environment is difficult to navigate; learning pathways are inflexible.

In practice: Core files are scanned or image-only, videos lack accurate captions and Ultra navigation changes from week to week.

Developing

Some movement

Some accessibility features exist but inconsistently; some choice is offered in isolated tasks rather than designed systematically across the course.

In practice: Some materials pass accessibility checks, but accessibility depends on the individual staff member and alternatives are created reactively.

Proficient

Strong practice

Most materials meet accessibility standards; the online environment is clearly structured; students have meaningful choices in at least some ways of engaging and demonstrating learning.

In practice: Accessible source files, consistent navigation and several purposeful learning pathways are built into most of the course.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Materials are proactively designed for accessibility from the outset; multiple formats and pathways are standard; students have genuine agency over topics, modes of engagement and ways of learning.

In practice: Accessibility is part of templates, commissioning, procurement and quality assurance, while students have meaningful agency over learning pathways.

No/Poor → Developing
  • Fix one high-use document at the source using headings, real text, descriptive links and logical reading order.

  • Standardise the naming and sequence of weekly Ultra content.

Developing → Proficient
  • Create reusable accessible Word, PowerPoint and Ultra templates.
  • Add lower-bandwidth or alternative access to essential information.
  • Test a complete student task at high zoom and with keyboard navigation.

Proficient → Excellent

  • Create an escalation and fallback process for inaccessible third-party tools.

  • Share templates, test results and recurring platform issues across the program.

L

Language and literacies

How clearly the course scaffolds academic language, discipline conventions and the literacies required for success.

No/Poor

Baseline gap

Academic and disciplinary language is assumed throughout; there is no explicit scaffolding for EAL students or others managing language processing or prior learning gaps.

In practice: The course assumes students already understand disciplinary vocabulary, academic genres, assessment conventions and placement communication norms.

Developing

Some movement

Language diversity is acknowledged but scaffolding is inconsistent or supplementary; placement preparation gives limited attention to professional language expectations.

In practice: A glossary or support link exists, but language development is separate from core learning and appears only when students struggle.

Proficient

Strong practice

The course explicitly connects learning to academic and disciplinary language; key terminology and literacy foundations are anticipated and scaffolded.

In practice: Terminology, genres and hidden conventions are explicitly introduced, practised and revisited through the course.

Excellent

Embedded practice

Language development is treated as core learning; terminology and expectations are unpacked throughout; unnecessary complexity is removed and multiple language backgrounds are supported without requiring disclosure.

In practice: Language and literacy development are integrated across the program, unnecessary complexity is removed and varied language backgrounds are treated as assets.

How to move this area forward

No/Poor → Developing
  • Highlight unexplained jargon, figurative language and assumed academic conventions in one module.
  • Create a short glossary linked to the learning activity.
  • Show an annotated example of the genre or professional communication expected.

Highlight unexplained jargon, figurative language and assumed academic conventions in one module. Create a short glossary linked to the learning activity. Show an annotated example of the genre or professional communication expected.

Developing → Proficient
  • Sequence opportunities to practise disciplinary language before high-stakes use.
  • Explain why conventions matter and which are genuinely essential.
  • Review instructions with students unfamiliar with the discipline.

Sequence opportunities to practise disciplinary language before high-stakes use. Explain why conventions matter and which are genuinely essential. Review instructions with students unfamiliar with the discipline.

Proficient → Excellent
  • Map language and literacy development across the program.
  • Coordinate terminology and expectations across courses.
  • Treat feedback on language as part of learning rather than only correction.

Map language and literacy development across the program. Coordinate terminology and expectations across courses. Treat feedback on language as part of learning rather than only correction.

Maintain, evaluate and share
  • Evaluate whether multilingual and varied communication practices can be recognised more fully.
  • Share successful scaffolds across disciplines and placement settings.
  • Continue removing complexity that does not serve the learning outcome.

Evaluate whether multilingual and varied communication practices can be recognised more fully. Share successful scaffolds across disciplines and placement settings. Continue removing complexity that does not serve the learning outcome.
